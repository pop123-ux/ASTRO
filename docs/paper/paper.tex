\begin{abstract}
Matrix-structured optimizers---Muon, Shampoo, SOAP, NorMuon---improve on AdamW by
treating a weight matrix as a linear operator rather than a bag of coordinates.
We report a controlled study of what in these methods is responsible for the
gain, under a protocol with equal tuning budgets, multiple seeds, exact paired
rank tests, Holm correction, and wall-clock matching.

Two findings are structural rather than statistical.

\textbf{Leverage.} For a momentum matrix $M = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ with $m > n$, the
squared row norms of Muon's update are exactly the leverage scores of $M$'s row
space, summing to $n$. Two consequences follow that appear unremarked. For wide
matrices the polar factor has orthonormal rows, so any row-normalisation scheme
is provably inert there---a statement about NorMuon's domain of applicability
that NorMuon does not make. And on a \emph{fused} query/key/value projection the
leverage localises onto whichever stacked operator has the largest gradient,
because $Q$ and $K$ reach the loss through the softmax Jacobian while $V$ enters
linearly. The tensor passes every orthogonality check one would apply while two
of the three operators inside it barely move.

We measure this on eight pretrained checkpoints from 124M to 1.4B across two
fusion layouts. It is an \emph{initialisation-time} phenomenon that attenuates:
$V$'s share of the update is $0.65$ at random initialisation ($0.76$ on the GPT-2
family) against $0.50$ on the trained weights, where uniform is $0.333$. This
independently explains a stage dependence reported in the literature, that
head-wise splitting helps early and is overtaken by full-matrix Muon later.

\textbf{Non-convergence.} Muon's quintic is one polynomial applied $k$ times, and
it cannot reach the polar factor at any budget: its fixed points solve
$2.4445 - 4.7750s^2 + 2.0315s^4 = 0$, at $s = 0.868$ and $s = 1.264$. Singular
values land in a band around them, and \emph{where} in that band depends on the
conditioning of the matrix handed over, so the update's Frobenius norm moves
between $0.688$ and $0.921$ of its theoretical value as the condition number goes
$1$ to $1000$---an uncontrolled drift in effective step size tracking a quantity
nobody monitors. Solving one polynomial per step greedily removes the
obstruction; at equal cost the drift halves and centres on $1$, and two steps
further it is exact.

We report six claims this work made and withdrew and three bugs in our own
measurement code, all of which produced publishable-looking numbers. Most were
caught by a control disagreeing with a headline, one by an arithmetic check, one
by re-running a seed we had assumed was reproducible, and none by reading the
code. We argue this is the more transferable contribution:
the standard fairness recipe of equal tuning budgets is necessary and
demonstrably not sufficient---we violated its spirit three times while
satisfying its letter, twice by pairing an update scale with the wrong
learning-rate range and once by counting a fixed value as a tuned dimension.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\citet{wen2025fantastic} benchmarked ten optimizers against a properly tuned
AdamW and found the real speedups near $1.1\times$ rather than the $2\times$
commonly claimed. The gap was methodological: unequal tuning budgets and
single-seed reporting. Any new optimizer result therefore inherits an obligation
to make its comparison hard to rig and to publish the protocol alongside the
numbers.

This paper is an attempt to meet that obligation, and a report of what happened
when we did. We set out to build an optimizer that beats the state of the art in
the regime where matrix methods are documented to lose---fully fine-tuning
Adam-pretrained weights \citep{qu2026muon}---and that attempt failed. What the
work produced instead is a set of findings about \emph{why} these methods work,
one structural defect present in all of them, and a case study in how a
carefully-designed protocol still admitted two errors.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The leverage identity} (\S\ref{sec:leverage}): the row-norm profile
of a Muon update is the leverage-score distribution of the momentum's row space.
Elementary, but it implies row normalisation is inert on wide matrices and
explains why a fused projection starves two of its three operators.
\item \textbf{The stage dependence of the fused-projection defect}
(\S\ref{sec:qkv}), measured on eight pretrained checkpoints with a random-init
control. The split itself is \emph{not} ours---production Muon implementations
already split per slice, and head grouping is published. What is ours is the
mechanism and the finding that the defect decays with training.
\item \textbf{A converging polar iteration} (\S\ref{sec:polar}), solved rather
than copied. Muon's quintic has fixed points at $0.868$ and $1.264$ and so
cannot converge; per-step coefficients can. The solved tail reproduces the
published Polar Express asymptote, which was not fitted to.
\item \textbf{A weight-tying routing bug} (\S\ref{sec:routing}) that places the
tied token embedding and the position embedding on the spectral path while
excluding a genuine MLP projection, affecting every matrix optimizer using the
standard name-based policy.
\item \textbf{A component whose sign inverts with scale} (\S\ref{sec:inversion}).
Cautious masking is worth $-0.0291$ against Muon on 8 of 8 seeds at 1.17M
parameters, exact $p = 0.0078$, and costs $+0.1341$ at 124M under the same
tokeniser and protocol. We built the small benchmark specifically to match the
target's parameter split, which removed our own explanation for the inversion
without removing the inversion.
\item \textbf{Six retractions and three measurement bugs}
(\S\ref{sec:corrections}), reported because the pattern is instructive and
because one of them---that splitting is cheaper than not splitting---was a
reasoning error we published before checking the arithmetic.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}

Let $W \in \R^{m\times n}$ be a parameter reshaped to the operator it represents,
$G = \nabla_W \mathcal{L}$, and $M$ an exponential moving average of $G$. For
$A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ the \emph{matrix sign} or polar factor is
$\msgn(A) = UV^{\top}$, the nearest orthogonal matrix to $A$. Steepest descent
under a norm $\norm{\cdot}$ solves
$\arg\min_Q \langle -G, Q\rangle + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\norm{Q}^2$, and under the
spectral norm the solution is proportional to $\msgn(G)$
\citep{bernstein2024modular}. This is the sense in which Muon
\citep{jordan2024muon} is a duality map rather than a heuristic: it replaces the
update with one whose singular values are all one, so the step moves equally in
every direction the gradient identifies.

\citet{frans2025whitening} decompose matrix whitening into \emph{spectral
normalisation} and \emph{variance adaptation}, and identify the second as what
explains SOAP's \citep{vyas2024soap} edge over Muon. NorMuon
\citep{li2025normuon} adds a neuron-wise second moment \emph{after}
orthogonalisation, reporting that ``Muon's updates exhibit highly non-uniform
neuron norms, causing certain neurons to dominate.'' Section~\ref{sec:leverage}
makes that statement exact and shows where it does and does not apply.

\section{The leverage identity}\label{sec:leverage}

\begin{proposition}[Row norms are leverage scores]\label{prop:leverage}
Let $M = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ with $m > n$, so $U \in \R^{m\times n}$ has orthonormal
columns, and let $P = \msgn(M) = UV^{\top}$. Then for every row $i$,
\[
\norm{p_i}^2 = \norm{u_i}^2, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^m \norm{u_i}^2 = n,
\]
where $p_i, u_i$ are the $i$th rows of $P$ and $U$. That is, the squared row norms
of the update are the leverage scores of $M$'s row space, summing to $n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$p_i = u_i V^{\top}$ and $V$ is orthogonal, so $\norm{p_i} = \norm{u_i}$. The sum
is $\operatorname{tr}(U^{\top}U) = \operatorname{tr}(I_n) = n$.
\end{proof}

Two corollaries follow immediately and are, to our knowledge, unremarked.

\begin{remark}[Row normalisation is inert on wide matrices]
For $m \le n$ the polar factor has orthonormal \emph{rows}, so every row norm is
exactly $1$ and any row-normalisation scheme is the identity. NorMuon's
mechanism therefore cannot act on wide matrices at all. On GPT-2's
$128\times512$ MLP projection we measure a participation ratio of $0.995$
(Table~\ref{tab:leverage}), confirming this empirically.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Localisation is a signal, not a defect, except when it is]
For a pure-noise matrix, leverage scores are delocalised, concentrating near
$n/m$ by Marchenko--Pastur. Structured signal localises them. Row normalisation
therefore pushes the update toward the profile that noise would produce, which is
defensible early in training when leverage estimates are unreliable, and is what
we believe underlies NorMuon's gain from scratch.
\end{remark}

\section{A polar iteration that converges}\label{sec:polar}

Muon's quintic \(p(s) = 3.4445s - 4.7750s^3 + 2.0315s^5\) is applied \(k\) times.
It cannot reach the polar factor at any \(k\): its fixed points solve
\(2.4445 - 4.7750s^2 + 2.0315s^4 = 0\), giving \(s = 0.868\) and \(s = 1.264\),
and iterating lands in a band around them. We measure singular values in
\([0.68, 1.13]\) after five steps and unchanged after twelve.

The cost is not cosmetic. Where in that band the spectrum lands depends on the
conditioning of the matrix handed over, so the update's Frobenius norm moves
between \(0.688\) and \(0.921\) of \(\sqrt{\min(m,n)}\) as the condition number
goes \(1\) to \(1000\)---a drift in effective step size that tracks a quantity no
implementation monitors and that moves as training reshapes the gradient
spectrum.

A different polynomial per step has no fixed-point obstruction. We solve for the
coefficients greedily, each step minimising \(\max_s |P(s) - 1|\) over the
interval the previous steps produced, rather than copying published constants.

\section{The fused-projection defect}\label{sec:qkv}

Table~\ref{tab:leverage} shows \texttt{c\_attn} at participation $0.433$, worse
than \texttt{c\_fc} despite being less tall. \texttt{c\_attn} is the fused QKV
projection: three separate operators stacked in one $3d \times d$ tensor. A
participation ratio near $1/3$ suggests one of the three dominates. It does,
and Table~\ref{tab:qkv} measures by how much.

This defeats the property Muon exists to provide. The tensor is perfectly
orthogonalised---its singular values are all one---while two of the three
operators inside it receive almost no update. No orthogonality diagnostic
detects this, because nothing is wrong with the orthogonalisation; what is wrong
is that the object being orthogonalised is not a single operator.

\paragraph{The fix.} Split the tensor into its blocks and orthogonalise each
separately. On a planted-gradient test with $Q,K$ scaled to $2\%$ of $V$, the
update mass moves from $(0.019, 0.018, 0.963)$ to $(0.336, 0.334, 0.330)$ at
unchanged overall update norm.

\paragraph{The fix costs more, not less.} We first reported that splitting is
\emph{cheaper} than not splitting, on the reasoning that three $128\times128$
Newton--Schulz iterations must cost less than one $384\times128$. That is wrong,
and the error is instructive because the arithmetic is one line. Newton--Schulz
transposes to operate on the smaller dimension, so a fused $(3d, d)$ tensor
already runs as $(d, 3d)$ at $7d^3$ per iteration, against $9d^3$ for three
separate $d\times d$ blocks. Splitting is $1.29\times$ more expensive by
operation count; we measure $1.33\times$ on a T4. The end-to-end step timings we
published ($91.0$\,ms against NorMuon's $92.2$) were real, but they measure our
step being cheaper than NorMuon's for unrelated reasons, and we attributed them
to a saving that does not exist.

Because the defect is an initialisation-time phenomenon that attenuates, the
right shape for the repair is a schedule rather than a permanent change: we
therefore relax to a single block after a configurable step count, which is also
the cheaper branch.

\citet{khona2026soap} mention QKV splitting as an implementation option and
explicitly disable it to keep their Muon/SOAP comparison free of structural
differences. The cost of not splitting appears unmeasured.

\section{A routing bug under weight tying}\label{sec:routing}

GPT-2 ties \texttt{lm\_head.weight} to \texttt{transformer.wte.weight}: they are
one tensor, so \texttt{named\_parameters()} reports it once, under the \texttt{wte}
name. A router keyed on names therefore never sees \texttt{lm\_head.weight}, and
the tied token embedding stays on the spectral path---orthogonalising a table
whose rows are indexed by token, which Muon's own guidance forbids. The position
embedding follows, being shape-indistinguishable from a dense matrix. The usual
fallback (``the last 2-D parameter is the head'') then selects the final block's
MLP projection, a genuine operator, and excludes it.

Three tensors misrouted in both directions, on the most widely used architecture
in the field, affecting Muon and NorMuon identically since they share the policy.
The fix is to resolve parameters by identity and to detect embeddings and the
output head structurally from the module graph.

\section{Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

Enforced in code rather than by convention: identical tuning budgets (16
log-uniform trials, same RNG stream per optimizer, with a hard error if
competitors do not tune the same \emph{number} of hyperparameters); tuning on
seed 0 with evaluation on disjoint seeds; paired exact Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
with the null distribution enumerated over the observed midranks; Holm correction
across each family; and wall-clock matching on an idle machine.

The model is nanoGPT's GPT-2 at $n_{\text{layer}}=4$, $n_{\text{head}}=4$,
$n_{\text{embd}}=128$, context $128$ (806K non-embedding parameters), trained on
WikiText-2 and fine-tuned on tinyshakespeare at character level with a shared
vocabulary. This is three orders of magnitude below the scale at which the
literature makes its claims, and optimizer advantages are known to shrink with
scale \citep{wen2025fantastic}; \S\ref{sec:limitations} states what that permits.

\begin{remark}[Equal budgets are not sufficient]\label{rem:ranges}
Our protocol enforces an equal \emph{number} of tuned hyperparameters. It does
not, and the standard recipe does not, require the \emph{ranges} to be
comparable. For matrix optimizers the update scale determines the correct
learning-rate range: an RMS-matched update needs Adam's range, a Muon-scaled
update needs Muon's, which is $10\times$ higher. We violated this without
noticing, and it cost a candidate $0.034$ nats---see \S\ref{sec:corrections}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Equal treatment is not neutral treatment]\label{rem:schedule}
The same failure has a second form, which we found by auditing a result that
looked too good. Applying one learning-rate schedule to every optimizer is
equal treatment, and it is not neutral: a constant rate penalises a method whose
late-training stability depends on decay more than it penalises one that
normalises its own update size. On tinyshakespeare, AdamW's tuned constant rate
sits immediately below a cliff---validation loss $2.214$ at $\eta = 2.35 \times
10^{-3}$ against $2.528$ at $3.5 \times 10^{-3}$---so its best available
configuration is one it cannot survive on every seed, and $6$ of $25$ seeds
land in a second mode near $2.45$. Granting nanoGPT's warmup-plus-cosine
schedule to everyone lets AdamW triple its rate to $6.99 \times 10^{-3}$,
removes all six outliers, cuts its seed-to-seed spread by $4.4\times$, and
improves it by $0.157$ nats. Our advantage over it fell by the same amount.

We therefore report under both settings rather than under the one we prefer. The
general point is that a shared experimental condition is only fair when the
optimizers are equally sensitive to it, which is not something equal budgets
establish and not something we can check without varying the condition.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The tuner has more variance than the effect}\label{sec:tunervariance}

The two remarks above are about making a tuned comparison fair. At 124M we
found that a tuned comparison is the wrong instrument regardless of fairness,
because the selection step moves the answer further than the optimizers do.

Three measurements, each at GPT-2 124M for $900$ steps on FineWeb-Edu. First,
tuning is worth $0.1184$ nats to Muon---more than twice the margin we were
attempting to measure at the time. Second, tuning at $300$ steps and evaluating
the selection at $900$ inverts NorMuon's ranking of two configurations: the one
the short-horizon search selects reaches $6.1301$ at $900$ steps and the one it
rejects reaches $5.9966$, a misselection worth $0.1335$. Third, the same
configuration re-run on a later runtime moves by at most $0.0012$. The
\emph{selection} therefore carries roughly a hundred times the variance of the
\emph{evaluation}, and no number of seeds addresses it, because seeds resample
the evaluation.

We consequently report a design with no selection step in it. Every optimizer
sharing a search space is offered the identical drawn configurations---common
random numbers, seeded from a fixed constant---and we pair on the configuration
rather than on the seed. The question becomes whether the advantage holds at
\emph{every} drawn configuration, which is strictly stronger than a
tuned-versus-tuned win: it cannot be a lucky draw, because nothing is drawn
twice and nothing is chosen. It also implies the tuned comparison, since a
maximum over a shared grid follows the pointwise order. Optimizers whose update
scale demands a different range (Remark~\ref{rem:ranges}) contribute no pairs
rather than mismatched ones; pairing is on the recorded configuration, never on
the trial index.

One further control makes the remaining budget informative. The multiplier on
the elementwise path---covering embeddings and the tied head, $31.7\%$ of GPT-2
124M---selects the learning rate for code that is \emph{identical} in Muon,
NorMuon, AdaMuon and our optimizer. It is not part of what distinguishes them,
and letting each search find its own value makes the comparison partly a lottery
over a shared subsystem: that lottery is the mechanism behind the $0.1335$ above.
We hold it fixed for all competitors at the value tuned for the \emph{baseline}
at the evaluation horizon, which biases against us, and spend the search density
on the matrix path instead.

\begin{remark}[Reproducibility is not a single number]\label{rem:noisefloor}
Re-running one configuration on a later runtime moved four optimizers by
$0.0009$, $0.0009$, $0.0010$ and $0.0012$. The same procedure at a learning rate
$4.9\times$ higher moved Muon by $0.0175$. A noise floor quoted without its
configuration is therefore not a property of the setup; we quote the
same-configuration figure of about $0.001$ and note that the floor degrades
where the optimizer is already struggling.
\end{remark}

$18\times$ that floor and the cost of the mask is $63\times$ it.

\subsection{A component whose sign depends on scale}\label{sec:inversion}

The row we removed is cautious masking \citep{liang2024cautious}, and it is the
most useful result in this section because of \emph{how} it failed.

On the 1.17M-parameter subword benchmark---same tokeniser, same protocol, same
parameter split as GPT-2 small to within $0.5$ percentage points---the mask is
worth $-0.0291$ against Muon on 8 of 8 seeds, exact $p = 0.0078$. That is as
clean as a small-scale result can be at any seed count we can afford. At 124M it
costs $+0.1341$: four times the magnitude, in the opposite direction.

We localised it before testing it. NorMuon's published advantage over Muon is
$0.0051$, and our configuration trailed NorMuon by $0.1044$---twenty times a
gap that ought to have been comparable---so the damage had to be in one of our
three additions rather than in the shared spectral machinery. The mask was the
prediction, and it was confirmed at three seeds. We note that we constructed the
subword benchmark specifically to match the target's parameter split, on the
hypothesis that the split was what the small benchmark was getting wrong. It
matched, and the inversion survived, which kills our own explanation.

The mechanism we can defend is narrower: the mask deletes coordinates whose sign
disagrees with the \emph{current minibatch} gradient and rescales the survivors
by $\text{numel}/\text{count}$. At larger width the momentum is the better of
the two estimates, so most of what the mask removes is the minibatch being
wrong rather than the update being wrong, and the rescale amplifies what
survives.

The transferable claim is not that small benchmarks are useless. This one
selected the QKV split, the variance placement and the update scale, all of
which held at $100\times$ the scale. It is that a component's \emph{sign} is not
among the things a $100\times$-smaller benchmark establishes, however many seeds
agree---and that an 8-of-8 sweep at $p = 0.0078$ is exactly what a wrong sign
looks like when the effect is real at the scale you measured it.

\subsection{The small-scale study}\label{sec:results-small}

\subsubsection{Component ranking}

On the fine-tuning ablation, cost of removing each component: update-RMS matching
$+5.3\%$; Hyperball norm control instead of weight decay $+4.5\%$; cautious
masking $+3.2\%$; hard anchor instead of elastic $+2.2\%$; dead-zone filter
enabled $+1.9\%$; variance adaptation $+1.1\%$; \textbf{the anchor $+0.0\%$}.

The largest single contributor is the component that exists for \emph{fairness}:
making a learning rate transfer between the spectral and scalar paths. An
evaluation that does not control for it credits step-size calibration to matrix
structure.

\subsubsection{Held-out validation}

On fine-tuning, held out from the candidate search: the QKV split remains
directionally better ($-0.0078$ vs AdamW, $d=-0.62$, $8/10$ seeds, Holm $0.42$),
while the full recipe ties both baselines ($d \le 0.10$, $p=0.92$). The split
transfers; the rest is regime-specific. Post-orthogonalisation placement wins from
scratch and pre-orthogonalisation wins when fine-tuning, consistent across every
comparison we ran.

\section{Corrections}\label{sec:corrections}

We record six withdrawn claims and three bugs, because the pattern---almost every
one caught by a control disagreeing with a headline, none by reading the code---is
the most transferable thing here.

\paragraph{A five-seed win that did not replicate.} A candidate beat AdamW on
fine-tuning by $-0.0111$ on $5/5$ seeds with a bootstrap interval below zero. At
ten seeds: $-0.0079$, $8/10$, exact $p=0.084$, Holm $0.168$. With $n=5$ the
smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is $2/2^5 = 0.0625$, so a perfect sweep is the
only impressive-looking outcome available, and is not unlikely for a small real
effect plus noise.

\paragraph{That claim then inverted on time.} AdamW's step costs $89$\,ms against
the candidate's $137$; in equal seconds it takes 120 steps to 78 and wins by
$+0.0315$.

\paragraph{``Muon beats NorMuon at equal wall-clock'' was wrong.} It rested on a
single per-step cost calibration that measured $80.2$\,ms in one run and $84.1$ in
the next, moving Muon's allocation from 457 steps to 438 and with it the entire
conclusion. Corrected: $+0.0007$, $d=0.04$, $p=1.0$.

\paragraph{A raw mean read as a result.} We described the from-scratch winner as
``worse than AdamW'' on held-out fine-tuning from a $0.0016$ difference. The
paired test gives $d=0.07$, $p=0.92$: a tie.

\paragraph{``Splitting is cheaper than not splitting.''} Withdrawn in
\S\ref{sec:qkv}. It is $1.29\times$ more expensive by operation count and
$1.33\times$ measured. This is the only one of the six that a control could not
have caught, because it was never a measurement---it was a one-line arithmetic
claim asserted from a plausible mental picture and published without being
checked. It is correspondingly the worst.

\paragraph{``Runs are deterministic given a seed.''} Our GPU reports said so, and
that claim is what licensed comparing a number from one session against a
baseline recorded in another. Re-running two identical seeds in a fresh session
moved them by up to $0.0021$. cuDNN autotuning and non-deterministic backward
reductions are enough to break bit-reproducibility even with every seed fixed.
The conclusions survive---the margin over Muon is $18\times$ this spread---but
several comparisons we would have been willing to make at $0.003$ were never
admissible.

\paragraph{Bug 1: ranges, not counts.} See Remark~\ref{rem:ranges}. Exposed by the
NorMuon replica missing its target by $0.034$.

\paragraph{Bug 2: configuration resolved by name, not recency.} The wall-clock
harness globbed candidate artifacts alphabetically, selecting a superseded
configuration and producing an apparent regression that was purely wrong
hyperparameters. A $0.027$ swing from a $0.9\%$ per-step cost difference is not
arithmetically possible, which is what prompted the check.

\paragraph{Bug 3: a paired test across unpaired seeds.} The candidate harness
evaluated a new configuration on seeds $200\ldots$ and compared it against a
field evaluated on seeds $100\ldots$, then ran a paired signed-rank test on the
two vectors. A paired test on unpaired samples is not a conservative
approximation of anything; it is a different test with the wrong null. The fix
derives the candidate's seeds from the reference's recorded seeds so pairing
holds by construction, and refuses to run rather than silently unpairing---and
the refusal happens before tuning, since the first version of the guard burned
ten minutes of search before declining to report it.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The largest gap is seeds at scale. Table~\ref{tab:124m} rests on three, which is
the exact sign test's floor at $p = 0.25$, so the 124M result cannot be certified
however clean it looks; and the configuration that wins it was never tuned, while
every baseline it beats was. Both gaps bias against the reported margin, which
makes it a lower bound rather than an estimate, but neither is a substitute for
running the sweep.

The second gap is horizon and size. Every number here is 300 steps at 124M or
below---far short of a Chinchilla-optimal budget---and optimizer advantages are
documented to shrink with scale \citep{wen2025fantastic}. We have no evidence
about 355M, 774M, or about whether a $0.04$-nat lead at 300 steps survives at
2700. That study is specified and instrumented in the repository; it is not run.

The small-scale study carries its own limits: character-level tokenisation puts
$96\%$ of the model on the spectral path against GPT-2's $68\%$, sixteen trials
over three dimensions leaves every method somewhat under-tuned, and the
from-scratch task is in-sample after three rounds of iteration on it. Per
\S\ref{sec:inversion} we now hold that its component \emph{rankings} transferred
and one component's \emph{sign} did not, and we cannot say in advance which kind
of quantity a new component is.

Finally, attribution. Cautious masking is \citeauthor{liang2024cautious}'s and we
removed it; the QKV split is not ours either---production Muon implementations
already split per slice. Our contributions are the leverage identity and the
mechanism it explains, the solved converging iteration, the scale inversion, and
the protocol.

\section{Conclusion}

The row norms of a Muon update are leverage scores. That elementary identity
implies row normalisation cannot act on wide matrices, and that a fused QKV
projection hands $85\%$ of its update to $V$ while passing every orthogonality
check. Splitting the projection fixes it, at a real cost of $1.33\times$ on the
orthogonalisation, and the defect it repairs decays with training---so the
repair belongs on a schedule.

The assembled optimizer beats Muon, NorMuon and AdamW at GPT-2 124M on three of
three shared seeds, at Muon's wall-clock. We call that directional rather than
established: three seeds floor the exact sign test at $p = 0.25$, and the
winning configuration is the one row in the table that was never tuned.

We consider the methodological findings at least as important. Equal tuning
budgets are necessary and not sufficient; ranges must match the update scale. A
component's sign does not survive a $100\times$ change in scale even when 8 of 8
seeds agree at $p = 0.0078$. And in a project explicitly designed to avoid
manufacturing results, three measurement bugs and six wrong claims still
occurred, almost every one caught by a control rather than by inspection.
Controls that can fail are the load-bearing part of an optimizer comparison.
